The basics

Eastern Orthodoxy is one of the oldest branches of Christianity, and Greek Orthodoxy is one of its best-known expressions.

It’s a communion of self-governing churches that share the same faith and worship, often named for the place they serve: Greek, Russian, Serbian, Romanian, Antiochian and others. The Ecumenical Patriarch of Constantinople holds a place of honor among them as “first among equals,” rather than as a pope-like ruler.

Eastern OrthodoxyThe shared faith: the Trinity, the Incarnation, the sacraments, the early Councils.
Greek OrthodoxThe Greek-speaking branch, with its own language, hymns and customs. In the U.S., the Greek Orthodox Archdiocese of America is under the Ecumenical Patriarchate.
Greek identityCulture and faith are deeply interwoven, but not interchangeable. Plenty of Greeks aren’t religious, and plenty of Orthodox Christians aren’t Greek.

Greek matters because the New Testament was written in Greek, and many early theologians wrote in it. Much of the Church’s vocabulary comes straight from there, including Eucharist (“thanksgiving”) and Orthodoxia, often explained as “right glory” or “right belief.”

A little history

Apparently, this tradition has been around a little longer than most of my hobbies. Tap each moment to open it.

1st c.Christianity begins in a Greek-speaking world
Paul’s letters to Corinth, Thessalonica and Philippi went to Greek-speaking communities, and the Gospels and letters were written in Greek. The faith spread along Mediterranean trade routes.
325–787The seven Ecumenical Councils
Bishops gathered to settle big questions, such as who Christ is. Orthodoxy recognizes the first seven. Nicaea (325) and Constantinople (381) gave us the Nicene Creed, still recited in Orthodox worship, and the seventh, Nicaea II in 787, affirmed the veneration of icons.
330Constantinople and the Byzantine Empire
Constantine dedicated his new capital in 330. For roughly a thousand years it was the center of a Greek-speaking Christian empire. The great church of Hagia Sophia was completed under Justinian in 537.
1054The Great Schism
In 1054 a papal legate and the Patriarch of Constantinople excommunicated each other. Historians stress that this symbolizes a slow drift rather than one clean break. Disputes over papal authority, the wording of the Creed, language and politics built up over centuries, and the 1204 sack of Constantinople in the Fourth Crusade deepened the wound. In 1965 the mutual excommunications of 1054 were lifted.
1453The Ottoman period
Constantinople fell in 1453. Under Ottoman rule, the Orthodox Church continued and, for many communities, became an important keeper of language, education and identity. Experiences varied a great deal by place and era. Greece’s War of Independence began in 1821, and the Church of Greece was declared self-governing in 1833.
TodayA worldwide community
Waves of Greek emigration built parishes across North America, Australia and beyond. Many serve as both a church and a cultural home.

What matters most

Core beliefs in plain language. Orthodoxy isn’t a variation on Catholicism or Protestantism, so even familiar words can mean different things.

The Holy TrinityOne God in three persons
Father, Son and Holy Spirit: one God, three distinct persons who are equal and eternally in relationship. In Orthodox thought, God is fundamentally communion and love, not a solitary power.
Jesus Christ and salvationGod entering the world
The Son of God became fully human while remaining fully God, joining God and humanity in one person. His death and Resurrection are the heart of Orthodox faith and worship; every Sunday is, in a sense, a small Pascha. Salvation is often described as healing and restored communion with God, a lifelong journey rather than a single moment.
Scripture and Holy TraditionNot one or the other
Scripture sits within Holy Tradition, meaning the Church’s lived life: the Councils, the writings of the Fathers, worship and the sacraments. The Bible is read in the Church’s own context rather than alone.
Saints and iconsWindows, not idols
Saints are people seen as living close to God, and Orthodox Christians ask for their prayers as one might ask a friend. That honor, called veneration, is different from worship, which belongs to God alone. Icons aren’t just religious art. Because God became visible in Christ, an icon is understood as a window onto the holy and a way of praying with the eyes. They’re painted according to long tradition, which is why they look so timeless.
The Divine Liturgy and EucharistThe center of worship
The Divine Liturgy, most often that of St. John Chrysostom, is the main service. The bread and wine are understood to become the Body and Blood of Christ, and Communion is received by the faithful. How that happens is generally called a mystery rather than explained.

A beautiful idea: theosis

If I had to pick the idea that surprised me most, it’s this one.

Theosis means union with God, sometimes called deification. The idea is that God became human so that humans could share in God’s life. Early Church Fathers such as Athanasius taught this, and Orthodox writers often point to 2 Peter 1:4, “partakers of the divine nature.” It isn’t a one-time event but a lifelong growth in love, humility and prayer.

Not becoming God by nature, but sharing in God’s life by grace.

Orthodox theology makes that distinction carefully: a person never becomes God in essence, but can truly share in God’s life. A classic image: iron placed in a fire glows with the fire’s light and heat, yet remains iron. Try it:

Faith in everyday life

Observance varies a lot between people and families. Not every Greek person is religious, and not every Orthodox Christian does everything below. This is just the general picture.

Divine Liturgy and worshipChanting, incense, candles, icons
Services engage every sense: chanted hymns, traditionally without instruments, incense, candlelight and walls of icons. Worshippers commonly light a candle and make the sign of the cross on entering.
PaschaOrthodox Easter
The biggest celebration of the year. At a midnight service the church goes dark, then candlelight spreads through the crowd as the greeting rings out: “Christ is risen!” “Truly He is risen!” (Christos Anesti! Alithos Anesti!). Red-dyed eggs are often cracked against each other afterward. Pascha frequently falls on a different date from Western Easter, because of a different method of calculation.
Great Lent and fastingPreparation, not punishment
The forty days before Holy Week are a time of prayer and simpler eating, traditionally without meat and dairy. How strictly people keep it varies widely by family and person.
Prayer and icons at homeA small corner of the house
Many homes keep an icon corner with a candle or oil lamp, a place for daily prayer. Some people make the sign of the cross, right shoulder to left, at meaningful moments.
Family, parish and communityOften one and the same
Many parishes host festivals, language classes, dance groups and coffee hour. Baptisms, name days (celebrating the saint you’re named for) and holidays often bring extended families together.
Greek hospitalityPhiloxenia
Philoxenia, literally “love of the stranger,” is the Greek ideal of welcoming guests. I’m told it involves a lot of food, and that I should arrive hungry. Noted.
